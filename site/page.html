Can a machine-learning model forecast tomorrow’s electricity demand better than simple rules?

I tested one model, LightGBM, against simple baselines on — US balancing authorities (the operators that keep a region’s supply and demand matched). Each day at 10:00 local time it predicted every hour of the next day, from 2021 to October 2026, and was only ever shown data from before the forecast.

With the weather known at forecast time, LightGBM missed demand by — on average, against — for simply repeating yesterday. On the — BAs that also report a day-ahead forecast to EIA, that forecast was off by — against LightGBM’s — on the same hours. Handed perfect weather for the target day, which no real forecaster has, LightGBM falls to — and edges past it.

So: the model clearly beats simple rules, and it does not beat the operators’ own forecasts unless it is given tomorrow’s weather in advance.

What the weather switch means. Take a forecast made on Tuesday at 10:00 for Wednesday’s 24 hours. “Known at forecast time” lets the model see weather up to Tuesday 09:00, the day the forecast is made, which is all a real forecaster would have. “Perfect target-day weather” also shows it the weather that actually happens on Wednesday, the day being forecast. Nobody has that at 10:00 on Tuesday, so treat those results as a ceiling, not a score a live system could reach.

How big are the misses?

Average size of the miss as a share of actual demand, over every hour of the next day (mean absolute percentage error). Lower is better. Every model is scored on the same hours.

Perfect-weather results are an upper bound: a live system would have to use weather forecasts, which are less exact than observations.

How it was done

  • Target. Demand for each of the 24 hours of the next local day, forecast at 10:00 local time, so 14 to 37 hours ahead. Demand inputs stop at 08:00 and weather inputs at 09:00, so nothing from after the forecast leaks in.
  • Baselines. The same hour last week; the same hour yesterday; the average of the last four same weekdays; and a regression on hour of week, holidays and heating and cooling temperatures.
  • LightGBM. One model shared by all BAs, with demand scaled by each BA’s average over the training window. It uses recent demand and lags, the calendar and weather.
  • Validation. Walk-forward: trained on 2019 to 2020 first, then refit every quarter on everything earlier, and tested on the next quarter. Training stops two days before each test quarter begins.
  • Weather. Observed temperature, dew point and wind at airports near each BA’s load centers. “Known at forecast time” stops at 09:00 on the day the forecast is made. “Perfect target-day weather” adds the weather actually observed on the day being forecast, the day after, which a live forecaster would not have at 10:00.
  • Cleaning. The raw hourly series contain impossible values, such as a reading of about 2.1 billion megawatt-hours in PJM. A reading is set to missing if it is not positive or is outside 0.4 to 2.0 times the median of the previous 15 days. Raw data are untouched.
  • Day-ahead timing. Day-ahead markets close their bids between 05:00 and 11:00 local time, mostly around 10:00 to 11:00, so the 10:00 forecast time is typical but not guaranteed to be early enough for every BA. Moving it four hours earlier or two hours later changes LightGBM’s error by less than half a point and changes no ranking.

What to keep in mind

  • EIA’s forecast is whatever each BA reports as its own day-ahead forecast, with no common timing, so its information cannot be matched exactly to the model’s. It is missing or unreliable for three BAs, which is why some views score 17.
  • Error rises in 2025 and 2026 for every method, EIA’s forecast included. Much of it comes from SRP, and from EIA’s forecast getting worse in several BAs. LightGBM does not lose ground against the simple baselines once SRP and AZPS are set aside.
  • LightGBM runs a little low in BAs whose demand has grown past what it saw in training, such as ERCOT and AZPS.
  • Values are the latest revised ones, so backtests are easier than a live system would face. History starts in 2019, so extreme events are few.
  • The conclusions are about the model against baselines and against published forecasts. They say nothing about beating grid operators.
